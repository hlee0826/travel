# Korean Minhwa × Pet Portrait Design Guideline

## A repeatable visual system for consistent image generation

**Version:** 1.2  
**Purpose:** Generate faithful, immediately recognizable portraits of individual pets, rendered through the authentic visual language of Korean 민화 (minhwa). Portrait likeness is the primary purpose; traditional painting structure is the governing artistic language.

## 1. Executive direction

The target is **not “a pet photographed in front of Korean decorations.”** It is a hybrid painted world with two deliberately different levels of realism:

1. **The pet is the emotional and descriptive anchor.** Its face, body proportions, markings, eye color, ear shape, and fur direction remain faithful to the reference animal.
2. **The surrounding world follows minhwa logic.** Space is shallow and flattened; flowers, trees, rocks, birds, insects, clouds, and water are symbolic actors rather than a believable photographic landscape.
3. **The whole image feels painted on warm hanji.** Matte pigment, visible fiber, layered translucent color, fine brush texture, and restrained irregularity replace digital gloss and cinematic lighting.
4. **Every supporting motif carries a wish.** The picture should convey one clear blessing—longevity, protection, good news, love, prosperity, learning, or family harmony—rather than assemble “Korean-looking” ornaments at random.
5. **The tone is affectionate, dignified, optimistic, and lightly whimsical.** Avoid parody, costume-pageant styling, hyper-cuteness, and excessive royal decoration.

### 1.1 Portrait-first doctrine

This work is first and foremost a **commissioned portrait of a specific pet**. It is not a minhwa scene that happens to contain a dog or cat. The animal is the key character, emotional subject, highest-detail area, and unmistakable focal point.

The production hierarchy is:

1. **Individual likeness and recognition** — exact facial structure, markings, proportions, posture, and characteristic expression.
2. **Traditional Korean painting integrity** — minhwa composition, shallow space, hanji, line, pigment, barim, palette, and visual rhythm.
3. **Symbolic narrative** — motifs that express the selected wish.
4. **Decoration** — only details that support the first three levels.

If these priorities conflict, the higher level always wins. A branch may move, a butterfly may disappear, and a layout may change to protect the pet’s silhouette and identity. The pet’s face, markings, or proportions must never be distorted to accommodate a motif or a preconceived composition.

#### Portrait dominance rules

- The pet must be identifiable before the viewer notices the symbolism.
- The face and eyes receive the highest edge clarity, contrast, and descriptive detail.
- The pet’s silhouette must remain uninterrupted around the head, ears, muzzle, chest, and distinctive tail.
- Motifs may frame, answer, or direct attention toward the pet; they may not compete with or visually crown it.
- No decorative element may cross the eyes, muzzle, signature markings, paws, or other recognition anchors.
- The pose is chosen from the pet’s characteristic behavior first; the composition is designed around that pose.
- Traditionalization occurs through brushwork, material, palette, space, and contour—not by making the pet generic, naïve, or anatomically inaccurate.

**Decisive test:** if the motifs and inscription were removed, the remaining painted animal should still succeed as a distinctive portrait of that individual pet.

### North-star style sentence

> A faithful, finely brushed portrait of a beloved pet inhabiting a flat, auspicious Korean minhwa garden on warm mulberry paper, surrounded by a small, meaningful constellation of flora, fauna, and weathered natural forms.

### What the supplied contemporary references contribute

The supplied images show a recognizable modern adaptation:

- a naturalistically rendered dog or cat, usually full-body or three-quarter-body;
- a warm ochre hanji-like ground with very shallow depth;
- a single large botanical or arboreal anchor entering from an edge;
- two to four smaller butterflies, birds, flowers, rocks, or water motifs creating a loose diagonal;
- fine, hair-by-hair fur rendering, with the eyes as the sharpest point;
- muted mineral colors rather than neon primaries;
- a small vertical name/signature and red seal near a corner;
- substantial quiet space around the pet.

The artist interviewed in Bricks Magazine describes the same hybrid method: the pet is sketched from a client photograph, the digital canvas is given a hanji feeling, the eyes are developed first, and the fur is built strand by strand in layers. The artist also notes that the digital workflow parallels the traditional sequence of tracing, underpainting, **barim** gradation, and final ink lines. This guide generalizes those observations into an original system; prompts should refer to the visual properties, not ask for a direct copy of a living artist’s style.

## 2. Historical foundation: what makes the image read as minhwa

Minhwa is better understood as a **visual language of lived wishes** than as one uniform rendering style. Surviving folk and decorative paintings, especially from the later Joseon period, combine symbolic animals, plants, flowers, rocks, clouds, household objects, and mythical beings with practical functions: celebration, decoration, protection, prosperity, fertility, longevity, and moral aspiration.

### Essential visual principles

#### 2.1 Symbolic, not merely decorative

Objects are chosen for meaning. A pine tree is not generic foliage; it participates in a longevity or steadfastness narrative. A magpie is not filler; it brings welcome news. Peonies, lotus, cranes, deer, peaches, butterflies, water, clouds, books, and rocks likewise contribute to the picture’s wish.

**Rule:** select one primary wish and no more than three supporting motif families.

#### 2.2 Flat, unconventional space

Traditional examples often minimize recession and volume. Size and placement express importance more than measured perspective. A flower can be unusually large; a tree may enter abruptly from the edge; a bird may hover in an open field without atmospheric logic.

**Rule:** use overlapping planes and vertical stacking, not a photographic horizon with deep perspective.

#### 2.3 Strong silhouette and readable contour

Even where internal detail is meticulous, the main forms remain immediately legible. The pet’s outer silhouette must read from thumbnail size. Plants should form clear masses, not dissolve into generic foliage noise.

#### 2.4 Bold but controlled color

Historical sources describe bright, saturated color and strong strokes. The supplied contemporary references soften this into an aged, restrained mineral palette. The appropriate hybrid is therefore:

- muted warm ground;
- medium-saturation botanical color;
- one or two high-chroma accents;
- dark ink reserved for focal contours and the pet’s features.

#### 2.5 Humor, vitality, and affectionate irregularity

Minhwa can be spontaneous, witty, exaggerated, and unconventional. Do not sterilize the composition into perfect bilateral luxury-brand symmetry. Slight asymmetry, playful scale, expressive eyes, bent branches, and unexpected pairings are welcome.

#### 2.6 Matte handwork on paper

Traditional Korean painting uses ink and pigments on paper or silk. Hanji’s mulberry fibers, pigment layers, and matte surface are important visual cues. The image should not resemble oil impasto, glossy 3D rendering, anime cel shading, or a commercial watercolor clip-art set.

## 3. Style DNA: invariants and variables

Consistency comes from locking a small group of invariants and changing only declared variables.

| Category | Lock for every image | Change per portrait |
|---|---|---|
| Medium | Ink and layered mineral/plant-like pigment on warm hanji; matte finish | Slightly cleaner or more aged paper |
| Hero | One dominant, uninterrupted, immediately recognizable pet; face, eyes, and defining markings are highest detail | Species, breed, characteristic pose, expression, coat |
| Space | Flat/shallow spatial field; no photographic horizon | Edge placement of plants, rocks, water |
| Composition | Pet occupies 42–58% of canvas height; 25–40% quiet ground | Centered, slightly left, or slightly right |
| Motif logic | One primary blessing, max three motif families | Chosen symbolism and season |
| Detail hierarchy | Face > chest/forelegs > body > flora > small fauna > paper | Fur complexity and motif density |
| Color | Warm paper + ink brown + muted green + one main accent | Accent may be peony red, lotus pink, blue, or persimmon |
| Light | Diffuse, shadow-light, no photographic spotlight | Very gentle warm/cool bias |
| Line | Fine organic contour, tapered endings, visible hand irregularity | More delicate for cats, firmer for dogs |
| Text | Reserved blank inscription zone; text composited later | Pet name, dedication, seal design |
| Mood | Tender, auspicious, dignified, quietly whimsical | Serene, alert, joyful, regal, contemplative |

### Non-negotiables

- The pet must be identifiable before the image is judged for beauty.
- Portrait likeness always outranks motif placement, decorative density, or compositional convenience.
- The paper and pigment treatment must unify the pet and background.
- Symbolic motifs must be intentional and limited.
- The image must retain quiet space.
- No text should be trusted to the generative model; reserve the space and add lettering afterward.

### 3.1 Balance principle: traditional world, individual pet

The intended balance is not a uniform 50/50 blend. Traditional and contemporary qualities should control different parts of the image:

- **Traditional minhwa controls the world:** composition, spatial logic, symbolism, motif relationships, hanji, pigment behavior, palette, line rhythm, and emotional auspiciousness.
- **The individual pet controls the identity:** face geometry, markings, eye color, ear carriage, muzzle, posture, body proportions, tail, coat direction, and characteristic expression.

This prevents both common extremes: a photographic pet placed over decorative Korean wallpaper, and a generic traditional animal that no longer resembles the customer’s companion.

| Design component | Target treatment |
|---|---|
| Face geometry and markings | 85–95% faithful to the individual pet |
| Body proportions and characteristic pose | 80–90% faithful |
| Fur | 65–75% naturalistic observation, translated into traditional fine brushwork |
| Eyes and expression | Faithful, with only slight poetic emphasis |
| Composition and spatial logic | 85–90% traditional minhwa |
| Flora, rocks, insects, birds, and water | 80–90% traditionally stylized |
| Perspective | Fully shallow, stacked, and symbolic |
| Lighting | Diffuse and embedded in the paper; never photographic studio lighting |
| Surface | Fully unified as hanji, ink, translucent pigment, and barim |

**Working rule:** preserve the pet’s identity, traditionalize its rendering, and make the surrounding world unmistakably minhwa.

### 3.2 Two independent approval gates

Every portrait must pass both gates:

1. **Identity gate:** would someone who knows the pet recognize it immediately without seeing the name?
2. **Tradition gate:** if the pet were temporarily hidden, would the remaining composition, space, materials, and motifs still read as Korean minhwa rather than generic botanical illustration?

An image that passes only one gate is unfinished.

### Customer-facing intake language

Ask customers what they want the portrait to **wish for or celebrate**, instead of asking them to choose from a long list of unexplained objects. Recommended question:

> What should this portrait hold for your companion: long life and health, love and family, protection and good news, peace and healing, prosperity and celebration, wisdom and curiosity, or renewal and resilience?

Then offer an optional second choice:

> Is there one traditional motif you especially love—such as pine, peach, peony, lotus, plum blossom, butterfly, or magpie?

The studio should validate that the requested motif supports the selected wish. If it does not, explain the traditional association and offer a compatible alternative rather than silently assembling contradictory imagery.

The customer may also nominate one personal element—a favorite toy, collar color, garden flower, or place-related object. Translate it into the painting’s material language and keep it subordinate to the traditional motif system.

### Reference-photo requirements

Use:

- one clear, eye-level face reference;
- one full-body or seated reference showing proportions;
- one side or three-quarter reference showing markings and tail;
- neutral, natural light if possible;
- the pet’s characteristic expression, not merely the sharpest photograph.

Avoid using a single wide-angle phone photo taken very close to the muzzle; it distorts nose-to-head ratio. Do not combine references from very different ages, grooming states, or coat lengths unless those differences are explicitly reconciled in the identity sheet.

## 5. Composition system

### 5.1 Default geometry

- **Preferred formats:** 4:5 vertical, 3:4 vertical, or 1:1 square.
- **Pet position:** center or 4–8% off-center.
- **Pet scale:** 48–62% of canvas height for full-body; 60–72% for bust/three-quarter portraits. Use the upper end when facial recognition would otherwise weaken.
- **Eye line:** approximately 40–48% from the top edge.
- **Quiet paper:** preserve 25–40% as unoccupied or lightly washed space.
- **Grounding:** use a low rock, soil wash, grass tuft, lotus leaf, textile plane, or faint shadow-wash; never a hard photographic floor line.
- **Large motif:** one dominant tree/flower/leaf mass occupying 15–25% of the image, entering from an edge and remaining lower in contrast than the pet’s face.
- **Small living accents:** two to four total butterflies, insects, or birds. Unequal sizes; never evenly spaced.
- **Inscription reserve:** 6–10% of width near an upper side, with no critical details beneath it.

### 5.2 Visual movement

Construct a soft triangular or S-shaped path:

1. pet’s eyes;
2. nearest blossom, branch, or butterfly;
3. secondary motif at the opposite edge;
4. return through the pet’s chest or tail.

The surrounding motifs should **frame and converse with** the pet, not form a decorative wreath.

### 5.3 Three approved layout recipes

#### Recipe A — Botanical bracket

Best for: serene cats, seated dogs, gentle commemorative portraits.

- Pet centered or slightly right.
- Tall flowering branch enters from upper left.
- Smaller continuation of the same plant enters from lower right.
- Two or three butterflies bridge the empty field.
- Bottom edge has only a faint soil or grass wash.
- Most closely matches the black-cat reference in the screenshots.

#### Recipe B — Guardian pine

Best for: alert dogs, protection, loyalty, longevity.

- Crooked pine trunk enters from left or upper edge.
- Pine canopy creates a dark counterweight above the pet.
- Low rocks and stylized water/cloud bands create a shallow base.
- Optional single magpie placed above and away from the pet.
- Keep the pet in the lower middle and leave an open ochre sky-field behind the head.

#### Recipe C — Seasonal canopy

Best for: joyful pets, tropical foliage, spring/summer energy.

- One broad leaf or blossom canopy occupies the top 20–25%.
- Pet sits below it, slightly off-center.
- Low flowering plant anchors the opposite corner.
- Butterflies form a descending diagonal.
- No second large tree; the canopy is the sole major environmental anchor.

### 5.4 Compositional exclusions

Avoid:

- centered pet plus perfectly symmetrical flowers on both sides;
- circular floral wreaths;
- realistic garden paths, fences, buildings, or photographic depth;
- a crowded “where’s Waldo” field of every Korean symbol;
- motifs that tangentially touch the pet’s ears, eyes, or whiskers;
- large objects directly behind the head that read as a halo unless deliberately intended;
- exact mirroring, equal spacing, and repeated copy-pasted butterflies.

## 6. Rendering the pet

### 6.1 Fidelity hierarchy

Preserve these in order:

1. face geometry and eye spacing;
2. defining markings and color boundaries;
3. ear shape and orientation;
4. muzzle-to-skull ratio;
5. body proportions and posture;
6. tail shape;
7. coat length, curl, and directional flow;
8. small scars, gray hairs, or grooming details that carry identity.

The word “breed” is never an adequate identity description. Two pets of the same breed can differ in skull width, eye spacing, muzzle taper, blaze shape, ear carriage, chest depth, leg length, tail set, coat break, and habitual expression. Record those differences explicitly.

#### Recognition-anchor record

For every commission, identify five to eight features that must remain invariant across all generations:

```text
PRIMARY FACE ANCHOR:
EYE-SPACING / EYE-SHAPE ANCHOR:
EAR ANCHOR:
MUZZLE / NOSE ANCHOR:
MARKING ANCHOR 1:
MARKING ANCHOR 2:
BODY-PROPORTION ANCHOR:
POSE / BEHAVIOR ANCHOR:
TAIL ANCHOR:
```

#### Four likeness tests

1. **Face-crop test:** in a crop containing only the head and upper chest, the pet remains recognizable.
2. **Silhouette test:** in a flat single-color silhouette, the ears, head, body, and tail still reflect the pet’s characteristic shape.
3. **Marking-map test:** the position, scale, and edge character of defining patches match the approved reference map.
4. **Familiar-person test:** someone who knows the pet identifies it without seeing the name, collar, background, or motifs.

Failure of any test returns the work to the identity stage. Traditional styling cannot compensate for a failed likeness.

### 6.2 Eyes

The eyes are the sharpest and most emotionally active area.

- Preserve exact eye color; avoid default amber.
- Use a small, soft highlight, not glossy anime sparkle.
- Keep both eyes anatomically aligned with the head turn.
- Outline lids with warm charcoal-brown rather than absolute black.
- Build the iris with two or three translucent tonal rings.
- Do not enlarge eyes beyond roughly 5–8% from the reference proportions.

### 6.3 Fur method

The modern reference style depends on fur built from many fine strokes.

- Establish the large coat masses first.
- Add underpainting in local coat color.
- Map fur direction by anatomical zones: forehead, cheeks, neck ruff, shoulders, barrel, limbs, tail.
- Add short tapered strokes in overlapping layers, varying hue and value.
- Reserve the finest individual strands for facial edges, ears, chest, paws, and marking transitions.
- Let body fur simplify slightly so the face remains dominant.
- Allow warm paper and underpaint to show through in selected gaps.

**Do not:** create uniformly sharp hair over the entire animal, airbrush the coat into plastic softness, or outline every strand with high-contrast black.

### 6.4 Cat-specific handling

- Emphasize whisker pads, delicate nose bridge, ear translucency, and the directional fan of whiskers.
- Render whiskers as sparse, confident curves; avoid a dense white scribble.
- Preserve flexible shoulder and paw structure.
- A seated cat may be slightly elongated to harmonize with vertical botanical forms, but identity proportions take priority.

### 6.5 Dog-specific handling

- Preserve breed-specific chest depth, leg length, muzzle type, ear carriage, and tail curl.
- For open-mouth expressions, keep tongue and teeth subdued; do not let a bright pink tongue become the focal point.
- Use slightly firmer contours around nose, paws, and chest than for cats.
- Long-haired breeds need grouped locks and directional clumps, not undifferentiated fuzz.

### 6.6 Pose and expression

Preferred:

- seated frontal or three-quarter pose;
- standing three-quarter pose with all legs structurally clear;
- head gently turned or gaze directed toward a butterfly/branch;
- relaxed mouth, alert ears, soft curiosity, quiet confidence.

Use running, jumping, rolling, or extreme foreshortening only as a separate energetic sub-series; these poses fight the still, iconic quality of the core system.

### 6.7 Light and volume

- Light is diffuse and ambient, as if embedded in the paper.
- Use gentle modeling on the pet, no hard cast shadow.
- Keep highlights broad and matte.
- Preserve enough volume to recognize anatomy, but avoid cinematic rim light, studio key/fill lighting, or deep chiaroscuro.

## 7. Motif language and symbolic selection

Meanings vary by period, context, and pairing. Treat the table as a practical art-direction vocabulary, not a claim that each symbol has only one permanent definition.

### 7.1 Customer-selectable “Wish Collection” strategy

Motifs should become a clear customization feature and product differentiator. Customers select a **Wish Collection**; the production system converts that intention into a historically coherent visual recipe.

Each collection contains five controlled parts:

1. **Wish:** the emotional or auspicious meaning selected by the customer.
2. **Anchor motif:** the largest symbolic element and main compositional structure.
3. **Supporting element:** a smaller motif that reinforces the wish.
4. **Movement element:** butterfly, bird, cloud, water, or falling petals that leads the eye through the composition.
5. **Visual recipe:** approved layout, palette, motif count, and restraint rule.

Customers may choose the wish and, where compatible, the anchor motif. The studio retains control of supporting and movement elements so the result stays visually balanced and culturally coherent.

#### Optional symbolism intensity

Offer three controlled density levels without changing the underlying style:

- **Quiet:** one anchor motif + one very small movement element; 35–40% quiet paper.
- **Balanced — default:** one anchor + one support + two or three movement elements; 28–35% quiet paper.
- **Ceremonial:** one anchor + two compatible supports + a more developed ground or canopy; 22–30% quiet paper. Reserve for large-format or milestone commissions.

“Ceremonial” does not authorize unrelated symbols, crowded collage, royal costume, or excessive gold.

### 7.2 Product tiers

The Wish Collection system can support three marketable levels:

1. **Curated Wish Portrait** — customer chooses one Wish Collection; the studio selects the approved motif bundle, palette, and composition.
2. **Signature Motif Portrait** — customer chooses a Wish Collection plus one compatible anchor motif and seasonal palette.
3. **Bespoke Story Portrait** — customer chooses a Wish Collection and contributes one personal element; the studio integrates it beneath the traditional visual hierarchy and presents a concept proof before final rendering.

The tiers add narrative and approval depth, not additional decorative clutter.

Every motif card should record whether its meaning is historically documented, broadly conventional, or a contemporary interpretive use. This prevents marketing copy from presenting flexible symbolism as an absolute historical fact.

### 7.4 Customer-to-art translation workflow

1. Customer selects one Wish Collection.
2. Customer optionally selects one compatible anchor motif, season, and symbolism intensity.
3. Studio reviews the pet’s appearance and behavior to choose the pose and layout.
4. System retrieves the approved supporting element, movement element, palette, and exclusions.
5. Studio produces a one-page concept card showing the wish, motif meanings, composition sketch, and color palette.
6. Customer approves the concept—not a menu of individual decorative objects.
7. The approved motif recipe is inserted into the master prompt and generation record.

This makes the symbolism visible and valuable to the customer while preserving the studio’s art direction.

| **Cloud / 구름** | Auspicious atmosphere, celestial transition | Protective or dreamlike scene | Sparse curled bands; keep low contrast |
| **Books / 책거리** | Learning, knowledge, aspiration | Clever/curious pet or family study | Use as a separate interior recipe; avoid mixing with a dense botanical garden |
| **Pair of birds** | Marital/family harmony and fertility in 화조도 contexts | Two-pet portrait or family gift | Pair above or beside subjects; species should match |

### 7.5 Motif-selection algorithm

1. Choose the owner’s intended blessing.
2. Select **one anchor motif** that states it.
3. Select **one supporting motif** that reinforces or softens it.
4. Add **one movement motif**—usually butterflies, a bird, cloud, or water.
5. Stop. Do not add a fourth symbolic family unless the composition is a deliberately dense 십장생도-inspired scene.

### 7.6 Approved motif bundles

- **Long life:** pine + peach + two butterflies.
- **Love and family:** peony or camellia + paired birds + butterfly.
- **Protection and good news:** pine + one magpie + rock/cloud, with the pet occupying the guardian role.
- **Quiet virtue:** lotus + rock + one pale butterfly.
- **Learning and curiosity:** reduced 책거리 shelf + brush pot + one flowering branch.
- **Renewal:** plum blossom + flowing water band + butterfly.

### 8.2 Color proportions

For the supplied-reference look:

- **60–72%** warm hanji and pale neutral field;
- **15–22%** pet local colors;
- **8–15%** greens and botanical middle tones;
- **3–6%** bright accent colors;
- **under 2%** very dark ink and seal red outside a black-coated pet.

### 8.3 Saturation and contrast rules

- Background chroma stays low.
- The pet’s eyes, nose, and one botanical accent carry peak contrast.
- No more than two accents above medium saturation.
- Black pets should not be rendered as flat black; use warm charcoal, deep brown, muted blue-black, and paper-reflected edges.
- White pets should not be pure white; build them from shell white, warm gray, pale ochre, and sparse cool shadows.
- Protect the paper tone from being color-corrected to bright white.

**Summer / vitality:** hanji light, pine green, jade leaf, peony red, gardenia yellow.  
**Autumn / longevity:** hanji shadow, bark umber, persimmon orange, muted chrysanthemum yellow, ink.  
**Winter / devotion:** pale hanji, warm ink, pine green, camellia red, shell white.

Use one seasonal palette per image. Do not combine all four.

## 9. Line, pigment, and surface

### 9.1 Paper

The ground should suggest sized hanji made from mulberry fiber:

- warm cream to pale ochre;
- very fine crossing fibers visible at close view;
- gentle tonal clouds and edge variation;
- no heavy stains, torn edges, coffee rings, or fake antique cracks;
- no repeating digital paper tile.

The paper is aged by tone and use, not distressed like a treasure map.

### 9.2 Underpainting and barim

Build forms in thin layers:

1. pale local color;
2. translucent midtone wash;
3. **barim**—soft controlled gradation toward petal bases, leaf edges, fur recesses, or rock contours;
4. selective deeper pigment;
5. final contour and fine detail.

Avoid one-pass airbrushed gradients. Barim should follow the form and show slight brush logic.

### 9.3 Contour

- Vary line weight from hairline to medium-fine.
- Use warm charcoal, brown-black, green-black, or color-matched outlines.
- Taper beginnings and endings.
- Allow tiny irregularities, but keep the pet’s eyes and muzzle controlled.
- Redraw important lines after coloring; do not leave every initial sketch line visible.

### 9.4 Plant rendering

- Build leaves as repeated but individually varied shapes.
- Paint veins selectively, not on every leaf.
- Let clusters form readable masses.
- Use darker outlines on the anchor branch and lighter outlines on distant leaves.
- Flowers should have two or three tonal layers and a clearly organized center.

### 9.5 Digital emulation settings

If the software allows layer control:

- paper texture: normal or multiply, subtle;
- underdrawing: multiply, low opacity;
- pigment layers: multiply/normal with translucent brushes;
- shell-white accents: normal or screen used sparingly;
- final ink: multiply, colorized warm black;
- texture overlay: under 15% opacity, masked away from eyes and nose;
- avoid global sharpening; sharpen locally around the face and selected fur.

## 10. Signature, calligraphy, and seal

The screenshots repeatedly use a small vertical inscription and a red seal. This is a strong compositional cue, but generated Korean text is unreliable.

### Workflow

1. In the generation prompt, request **“a reserved blank vertical inscription area”**, not actual letters.
2. Add the pet’s name or dedication manually after generation.
3. Use Hangul brush lettering or a restrained calligraphic hand; avoid faux-Chinese characters unless they are verified and meaningful.
4. Place one small square cinnabar seal below or beside the inscription.
5. Keep total lettering/seal area below 2.5% of the canvas.

### Placement

- upper right when the botanical anchor is left;
- upper left when the anchor is right;
- lower corner only when the upper field is compositionally busy.

Never place a signature across fur, an eye line, or a major blossom.

## 13. Repeatable generation workflow

### Phase 1 — Lock identity

1. Complete the identity sheet.
2. Choose the best face, full-body, and side/three-quarter references.
3. Record five to eight recognition anchors and the exact marking map.
4. Generate or draw an undecorated pet study first, on a plain warm hanji ground with no motifs.
5. Run the face-crop, silhouette, marking-map, and familiar-person tests.
6. Obtain identity approval before developing any symbolic environment.
7. Reject identity drift before adding motifs; never ask decorative refinement to solve a likeness problem.
8. Save the accepted pet description, recognition anchors, reference set, crop, seed, and model settings as the portrait’s identity lock.

### Phase 2 — Lock design

1. Record the customer’s chosen Wish Collection.
2. Validate the optional anchor motif against the collection.
3. Retrieve the approved supporting element, movement element, and exclusions.
4. Set symbolism intensity: quiet, balanced, or ceremonial.
5. Choose layout recipe A, B, C, or the reduced 책거리 variation.
6. Choose one seasonal palette.
7. Fix aspect ratio, pet scale, and quiet-space percentage.
8. Produce a concept card for approval.
9. Generate several low-cost composition drafts while keeping identity language unchanged.

### Phase 3 — Refine materiality

1. Select the strongest composition.
2. Strengthen hanji fiber, translucent pigment, and barim.
3. Correct duplicated insects and botanical tangencies.
4. Refine face, paws, tail, and marking edges locally.
5. Reduce global sharpness outside the face.

### Phase 4 — Finish

1. Composite verified calligraphy and seal.
2. Check value and color at thumbnail size.
3. Print-test on warm matte paper if physical output is intended.
4. Record final prompt, negative prompt, model/version, seed, aspect ratio, reference strength, and post-processing steps.

## 14. QA rubric — 100 points

Do not approve an image scoring below **82/100**, or any image that fails the identity gate.

### A. Pet identity and portrait success — 40 points

- Face geometry, skull/muzzle proportions, and eye spacing: 11
- Exact markings, coat colors, and marking-edge character: 9
- Ears, nose, eye color, and facial asymmetry: 7
- Body proportions and characteristic pose/behavior: 6
- Tail, coat structure, scars, gray hairs, and other individual details: 4
- Immediate focal dominance of the pet: 3

**Identity gate:** if a person familiar with the pet would hesitate to identify it, reject regardless of total score.

### B. Minhwa visual grammar — 20 points

- Shallow/flat symbolic space: 5
- Strong silhouettes and organic contours: 4
- Symbolic rather than random motif use: 5
- Affectionate asymmetry, vitality, and restraint: 3
- No generic pan-Asian visual substitutions: 3

### C. Composition — 12 points

- Pet scale, eye-line, and silhouette protection correct: 4
- Clear focal path returns to the pet’s face: 3
- 25–40% quiet paper preserved: 2
- Motifs frame rather than crowd or overlap the pet: 3

### D. Material and technique — 12 points

- Believable hanji surface: 3
- Matte layered pigment and barim: 3
- Fine tapered fur/linework that preserves identity: 4
- No plastic, glossy, or oil-painted finish: 2

### E. Color — 8 points

- Core palette followed: 2
- Accent count and saturation controlled: 2
- Pet remains distinct from background: 3
- Paper tone remains warm: 1

### F. Symbolic and emotional coherence — 8 points

- Primary wish can be explained in one sentence: 2
- Motifs support that wish without competing with the portrait: 2
- Artwork follows the customer’s approved Wish Collection and motif choice: 2
- Mood suits the individual pet and commission: 2

## 15. Common failure modes and corrections

| Failure | Cause | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Pet looks generic | Breed labels overpowered identity details | Move exact face/marking map into first paragraph; use stronger reference control |
| Motifs are noticed before the pet | Decorative contrast, scale, or density is too high | Reduce motif scale and saturation, clear the pet’s silhouette, and redirect movement elements toward the eyes |
| Pet resembles the breed but not the individual | Generic anatomy replaced recognition anchors | Return to the undecorated identity study; lock face geometry, marking map, asymmetry, and characteristic behavior before styling |
| “Korean” reads as costume or palace | Model relies on generic cultural shorthand | Explicitly exclude hanbok, crowns, palace interiors, temples; name hanji, barim, flat symbolic space, and chosen motifs instead |
| Background looks like watercolor wallpaper | Too little structural motif instruction | Name one anchor plant, entry edge, scale, one support, and exact small-fauna count |
| Too photorealistic | Camera and lighting language dominates | Remove lens/photography terms; specify matte ink and layered pigment on paper, no cast shadow |
| Too childish/cartoon-like | “Whimsical” or “cute” used too early | Lead with faithful anatomy and fine brush portrait; end with “gently whimsical” |
| Too sepia/antique | “Old paper” dominates | Use “warm clean hanji with subtle fiber,” ban stains/cracks, restore celadon/green/red accents |
| Flowers become a wreath | “Surrounded by flowers” is ambiguous | Use edge-entering botanical bracket with 30% quiet field and no circular arrangement |
| Symbol overload | Long motif inventory | Return to one blessing, one anchor, one support, one movement motif |
| Deep Western landscape | Horizon/perspective inferred | State “no horizon; vertical stacking; overlapping symbolic planes; shallow field” |
| Butterflies repeat | Model copies one token | Specify exact count, unequal size, different wing angles, irregular spacing |
| Black pet loses detail | Single black local color | Ask for warm charcoal, brown-black, muted blue-black, paper-reflected edges |
| White pet clips to blank | Pure white and bright background | Use shell white, warm gray, pale ochre, and celadon shadow; slightly darken paper |
| Eyes become anime-like | “Expressive” overemphasized | Preserve exact eye proportions; one soft highlight; no enlargement |
| Paws malformed | Motifs overlap limbs or pose is ambiguous | Clear ground around all paws; use reference pose; regenerate/local-edit before decoration |
| Text is garbled | Model-generated lettering | Reserve blank area and composite verified calligraphy afterward |
| Output feels like a direct copy of one artist | Over-reliance on screenshots | Build prompts from historical visual principles and the generalized system; vary motif bundle, layout, and palette while keeping invariants |

## 16. Cultural and creative safeguards

- Name the source tradition accurately as Korean **민화 / minhwa**; do not flatten it into “Asian vintage art.”
- Distinguish **historically documented meanings** from **contemporary artist interpretations**.
- Do not use sacred, royal, or funerary symbols purely as decoration when their context would conflict with the commission.
- When using a real artist’s work as a reference, describe transferable visual properties rather than requesting an exact imitation.
- Verify Korean calligraphy and seal text with a fluent reader before publication or sale.
- Preserve the pet owner’s emotional intent; symbolism should support the portrait, not turn it into an art-history demonstration.

## 17. Research basis and selected sources

### Traditional form, symbolism, and visual language

- [The Metropolitan Museum of Art — Birds and Flowers, 19th century](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/853895): birds, flowers, fruits, pine, crane, bamboo, lotus, and other motifs across seasons; auspicious meanings tied to romance, fertility, longevity, and virtue.
